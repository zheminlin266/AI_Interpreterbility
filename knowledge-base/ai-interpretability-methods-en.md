# What are the mainstream methods in AI interpretability today?

> **Sources checked through November 26, 2026.** This article focuses on the interpretability of machine learning models, using traditional models for tabular data and large language models (LLMs) as examples. “Mainstream” here means methods with an established research tradition, methods often used as baselines, or methods adopted by multiple research teams. It does not imply a ranking by deployment, publication count, or effectiveness. Recent exploratory directions are listed separately. Verification status: **The cited original sources were checked; the experiments were not reproduced in this repository.**

**Short answer:** To explain an individual prediction, input attribution or counterfactuals are usually a good starting point. If you can choose which model to use, consider evaluating an inherently interpretable option first. To explain an LLM’s internal computation, a common approach is to **find candidates with probes or sparse feature decompositions, test them with activation interventions, and then use circuit analysis to connect multiple computational steps**. These methods are not mutually exclusive. The key is to distinguish between “a signal is related to the answer,” “the model uses that signal,” and “we have explained the full mechanism.” [Sharkey et al., 2025, §1.1.1 and §2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16496v1#S2)

## A map of the methods: What can each tool tell you?

| Method | Main question and strengths | Limits: What the results cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inherently interpretable models**: sparse linear models, shallow decision trees, generalized additive models (GAMs/EBMs) | “What rules does the model use to score its inputs?” The rules or additive terms are part of the model’s own computation, so there is no need to approximate it with another model. These models make good baselines for structured data tasks. | Readability depends on the input features, the number of interactions people can make sense of, and the size of the model. A model’s rules are not real-world causal laws. Compare performance on the same data and metrics rather than assuming an interpretable model must lose to a black box. See [Rudin, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9122117/) and the [InterpretML EBM documentation](https://interpret.ml/docs/ebm.html). |
| **Input attribution, local surrogates, and saliency maps**: LIME, SHAP, Integrated Gradients (IG) | “Which inputs drove this prediction?” LIME fits a simple surrogate near the example; SHAP allocates the difference in prediction according to the specified background distribution and treatment of missing features; IG integrates gradients along a path from the baseline to the input. These methods can quickly point to fields, words, or parts of an image worth examining. | LIME depends on how perturbations and the neighborhood are defined. SHAP results are sensitive to the background data and how correlated features are handled. IG depends on the baseline and path and requires access to gradients. A visually convincing heatmap is not necessarily faithful, and it does not explain intermediate computations. Some saliency maps still look similar after the model parameters are randomized. See [LIME, §3](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.04938), [SHAP, §4](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.07874#S4), [IG, §3–4](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1703.01365), and [Adebayo et al.’s randomization tests](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.03292). |
| **Input counterfactuals**: finding alternative inputs that change the prediction | “How would the input have to change for the model to make a different decision?” Compared with importance rankings, counterfactuals show more directly what changes matter near the decision boundary. They can also offer several possible ways to change the prediction. | An input that is mathematically close may not be feasible in practice: some variables may be fixed or depend on one another. Changing the model’s decision does not mean the real-world outcome will change, nor does it reveal the full decision mechanism. See [Mothilal et al., 2020, §3 and §6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.07697#S6). |
| **Concept probes (probing)**: training a readout on the hidden states of a frozen model | “Can concept X be read out from a given layer?” With a clearly defined target concept and labels, probes can quickly compare layers, positions, and models, narrowing the search for follow-up interventions. | **Being able to read out a concept does not mean the original model uses it.** A high score may reflect shortcuts in the labels, word identity, or memorization by the probe itself. Compare with input baselines, random labels, or suitable control tasks, and use a separate intervention to test whether the model uses the concept. See [Hewitt and Liang, 2019, §2–3](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.03368#S2) and [Sharkey et al., §2.2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16496v1#S2.SS2.SSS3). |
| **Representation decomposition**: sparse autoencoders (SAEs) and related methods | “What patterns recur in the activations?” SAEs learn sparse features without predefined concept labels, using a small number of active directions to approximately reconstruct each internal activation. Researchers can then identify, label, and test candidate features. See [OpenAI’s GPT‑4 SAE research](https://openai.com/index/extracting-concepts-from-gpt-4/) and [Google DeepMind’s Gemma Scope](https://deepmind.google/models/gemma/gemma-scope/). | The dictionary is learned; it is not a unique inventory of the model’s actual concepts. One concept can split into multiple features or be absorbed into other features, and reconstruction error can leave out useful signals. **Better reconstruction at the same sparsity does not necessarily mean better performance on downstream interpretability tasks.** See [Karvonen et al., 2025, SAEBench §3–5](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09532v4#S4). |
| **Causal interventions and circuit analysis**: ablation, activation patching, and circuit tracing | “How does changing an internal state affect behavior? Which components work together to produce that behavior?” You can patch activations from a control example into a target run, remove components, or keep only a candidate circuit, then see whether the target output changes. Unlike correlational evidence, these interventions can test specific hypotheses about the mechanism. | Causal conclusions are limited to the intervention, model, and task studied. The choice of control example, patching method, and metric can change the results. Extreme changes can push the model into an unnatural state. A small circuit that preserves performance is not necessarily the only one, and this result does not establish that it covers other tasks. See [Zhang and Nanda, §3–6](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.16042v2#S6) and [Conmy et al.’s automated circuit discovery (ACDC)](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.14997). |

## The LLM frontier: Three directions worth following that are not yet general solutions

- **From what is represented to how it is computed.** An SAE typically reconstructs activations at the same location. A transcoder uses sparse features to approximate a component’s output from its input, and cross-layer variants can approximate MLP computations across multiple layers. Anthropic used these approximations to build attribution graphs and propose hypotheses about how Claude 3.5 Haiku performs tasks such as two-step factual reasoning, then went back to the original model to run interventions. **Attribution graphs come from a local surrogate model; they are not execution logs of the original model.** Using the same approach on a greater-than task in GPT‑2 Small, Goodfire recovered relevant features, but its account of the mechanism differed from what an earlier direct analysis had found. Together, the studies show the approach’s diagnostic value and why the graph alone is not proof. [Ameisen et al., 2025, methods and limits of faithfulness](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/methods.html#limitations-faithfulness); [Lindsey et al., 2025, two-step reasoning case study](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/biology.html#dives-tracing); [Goodfire, 2025, an independent team’s test on a different task and its limitations](https://www.goodfire.com/research/replicating-circuit-tracing-for-a-simple-mechanism#limitations-and-future-work).
- **Which computational units should we explain?** Sparse dictionaries are not the only option. Goodfire reported that, after screening and testing, the original MLP neurons in Qwen3.6-35B-A3B could also offer understandable clues. This is evidence from a specific model: it does not mean every neuron has only one function or that neurons can replace SAEs. Another approach is to change how models are trained. By restricting weight connections, OpenAI got the models in its study to form circuits that were easier to break down on simple tasks. In this case, sparsity is a property of the **original model’s weights**, which is different from training an SAE on the **activations** of an existing model. There are still limits in capability, training efficiency, and scaling the approach to frontier models. [Tigges et al., Goodfire, 2026](https://www.goodfire.com/research/neurons-as-an-interpretable-basis); [Gao et al., OpenAI, 2025, §3 and §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.13653#S5).
- **Translating internal activations into natural language.** Anthropic’s natural language autoencoder (NLA) encodes activations as text and then reconstructs the activations from that text. This helps people make sense of potential clues more quickly, but its training objective does not require the statements to be true. The authors report limitations including fabricated details, difficulty pinpointing specific internal mechanisms, and cost. This also differs from chain of thought (CoT) emitted directly by the model. CoT is observable text that can omit cues that changed the answer in controlled experiments, so it is useful as a lead for auditing, not as a complete record of the internal process. [Fraser-Taliente et al., 2026, Introduction / Limitations](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/nla/index.html); [Turpin et al., 2023, §3–4](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.04388); [Anthropic, 2025, Testing for faithfulness / Conclusions](https://www.anthropic.com/research/reasoning-models-dont-say-think).

## Four questions to ask when reading an interpretability paper

1. **What exactly are you trying to explain?** Specify the model version, behavior, input distribution, and output metric, and say whether the explanation is about an individual prediction, a statistical pattern, or an internal algorithm. When you only have API outputs, controlled comparisons of inputs are usually the first step. If you cannot read or modify activations, do not present behavioral results as evidence of an internal mechanism.
2. **How far does the evidence go?** Begin by checking whether the candidate factor can predict the behavior. Then test whether changing it affects the behavior as expected. If you claim to have found a circuit, ask, “Does removing it disrupt the behavior? Can keeping only that circuit reproduce the behavior? Are there alternative paths?” State the conditions of these tests in the conclusion.
3. **Are there controls that could disprove the explanation?** Vary the attribution baseline, prompt wording, activation patching method, or output metric. Compare against simple baselines and unrelated components, and use held-out examples to check effects and side effects. If the controls give different results, report those differences instead of picking the most compelling figure. [Zhang and Nanda, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.16042v2#S6); [SAEBench, §3.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09532v4#S3.SS5).
4. **How far can the conclusion generalize?** One model, one prompt, or one successful example supports only a conclusion of corresponding scope. Also report failures, unexplained contributions to the output, and performance across tasks or models. The authors’ own tests, checks with controls by other teams, and reproducing the results yourself are three different things. [Lindsey et al., Limitations](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/biology.html#limitations).
